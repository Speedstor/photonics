- Light travels in the form of rays. The rays are emitted by light sources and can be observed when they reach an optical detector
- An optical medium is characterized by a quantity $n\geq 1$, called refractive index. The refractive index $n=c_0/c$ where $c_0$ is the speed of light in free space and $c$ is the speed of light in the medium. Therefore, the time taken by light to travel a distance $d$ is $d/c=nd/c_0$. It is proportional to the product $nd$, which is known as the optical pathlength.
- In an inhomogeneous medium the refractive index $n(\mathbf{r})$ is a function of the position $\mathbf{r}=(x,y,z)$. The optical pathlength along a given path between two points $A$ and $B$ is therefore
$$
\text{Optical pathlength}=\int^B_{A}n(\mathbf{r})ds
$$
	where $ds$ is the differential element of length along the path. The time taken by light to travel from $A$ to $B$ is proportional to the optical pathlength
- Fermat's Principle. Optical rays traveling between two points, $A$ and $B$, follow a path such that the time of travel (or the optical pathlength) between the two points is an extremum relative to neighboring paths. This is expressed mathematically as 
$$
\delta\int^B_{A}n(\mathbf{r})ds=0
$$
	Where the symbol $\delta$, which is read "the variation of,",  signifies that the optical pathlength is either minimized of maximized, or is a point of inflection. It is, however, usually a minimum, in which case: "Light rays travel along the path of least time." Sometimes the minimum time is shared by more than one path, which are then all followed simultaneously by the rays.
